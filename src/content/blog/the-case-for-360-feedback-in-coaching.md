Everyone has blind spots. That's not a personality flaw. It's just how human perception works. You experience yourself from the inside. Everyone else experiences you from the outside. Those two pictures never fully match.

This is why 360 feedback exists. And it's why, done well, it's one of the most powerful things a coach can bring into an engagement.

Done badly, though, it's a political grenade. So let's talk about both sides.

## Why 360 feedback works in coaching

Self-assessment is unreliable. Research consistently shows that people overestimate their strengths and underestimate their weaknesses, or sometimes the reverse. Either way, self-perception alone is a shaky foundation for development.

360 feedback brings in other perspectives: managers, peers, direct reports, sometimes clients. The coachee sees how they're perceived by the people they work with every day. Not as judgement, but as data.

In a coaching context, this data becomes a starting point for real conversations. "Your direct reports rate you highly on technical expertise but lower on approachability. What do you make of that?" That's a question the coachee can sit with. It opens doors that self-reflection alone might never reach.

It also creates a measurable baseline. Run a 360 at the start of coaching, repeat it at the end, and you have concrete evidence of change. Not just "I feel more confident" but "the people around me have noticed a shift."

## Why people hate it

Let's be honest. 360 feedback has a reputation problem, and it's partly deserved.

**It gets used as a weapon.** Some organisations roll out 360s as a performance management tool, using peer feedback to justify ratings or decisions that have already been made. People learn fast. Once burned, they either game the system or refuse to engage honestly.

**Anonymity is fragile.** "Your feedback is anonymous" means less when you have a team of three and one of them clearly wrote that comment. Small rater pools make true anonymity almost impossible, and everyone knows it.

**The debrief is often terrible.** Handing someone a 40-page report full of numerical ratings and written comments, then leaving them to process it alone, is a recipe for defensiveness. Without skilled facilitation, 360 feedback can do more harm than good.

**It can feel like an ambush.** If a coachee doesn't understand why a 360 is happening, who will see the results, and how the data will be used, trust collapses before the first question is even answered.

## How to do it well

The good news is that every one of these problems is solvable. The key is in the setup.

**Make it developmental, not evaluative.** This is non-negotiable. If 360 feedback in your coaching programme feeds into performance reviews, promotions, or pay decisions, people will not be honest. The data becomes useless. Keep it strictly developmental. The results belong to the coachee and their coach. Period.

**Be transparent about the process.** Before launching a 360, explain to the coachee exactly what will happen. Who will be asked to provide feedback? What questions will they answer? Who will see the results? What happens to the data afterwards? No surprises.

**Let the coachee choose their raters.** Or at minimum, approve the list. This gives them ownership. They're more likely to trust feedback from people they selected than from a list imposed by HR.

**Keep the instrument focused.** A 360 with 80 questions and 15 competency domains is overkill for most coaching contexts. Pick the areas that matter for this person's development. Ten to fifteen well-crafted questions, with space for open comments, gives you more than enough to work with.

**Debrief with a coach, always.** Never just email someone their 360 results. The debrief conversation is where the real value happens. A skilled coach helps the coachee process the feedback, notice patterns, manage emotional reactions, and translate insights into action. This conversation is the whole point.

**Watch your rater pool size.** For meaningful anonymity, aim for at least three raters per category (peers, direct reports, etc.). If that's not possible, be upfront about it. "With only two peers responding, you may be able to identify who said what. Are you comfortable with that?"

## Timing matters

Run the first 360 early in the coaching engagement, ideally before or just after the first session. This gives the coach and coachee a shared foundation. Goals become more targeted. Sessions have more traction.

Repeat the 360 toward the end of the engagement, or a few months after it wraps up. This second data point is gold. It shows movement. It gives the coachee evidence of their growth. And it gives the organisation real data about coaching impact.

Some coaches resist the idea of a repeat 360 because it feels like a test. But framed correctly, it's a gift. "Let's see what the people around you have noticed." Most coachees are genuinely curious, especially if the first round led to meaningful changes.

## The political reality

Office politics don't disappear just because you're running a well-designed 360. People have agendas. Relationships are complicated. Someone might use the feedback process to settle a score.

You can't eliminate this entirely, but you can mitigate it. Keep rater pools broad enough that outlier responses don't dominate. Focus debrief conversations on patterns rather than individual comments. And remind coachees that feedback says as much about the rater as about the person being rated.

360 feedback in coaching isn't about getting a perfect picture. It's about getting a richer one. One that goes beyond how you see yourself and includes how others experience you. That's uncomfortable sometimes. It's also where the most important development happens.
