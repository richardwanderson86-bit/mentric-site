Ask most organisations how they measure coaching effectiveness and you'll get one of two answers: satisfaction surveys, or nothing at all.

Satisfaction surveys are easy to run and they produce nice numbers. "92% of participants rated their coaching experience as positive." Great. But did anyone actually change how they lead, communicate, or make decisions?

That's the harder question, and it's the one that matters.

## The problem with satisfaction scores

People generally like coaching. It feels good to have someone's full attention for an hour, to talk through challenges with a skilled listener. So satisfaction scores tend to be high across the board, regardless of whether meaningful change occurred.

High satisfaction and real behavioural change are not the same thing. A coachee can enjoy every session and still show up to their team meetings with the same habits they had before coaching started.

This doesn't mean satisfaction is irrelevant. If someone hates the experience, they won't engage, and coaching won't work. But satisfaction is a prerequisite, not a measure of success.

## Start with a baseline

You can't measure change without knowing where someone started. This sounds obvious, yet it's skipped constantly.

Before coaching begins, run a baseline assessment. This could be a self-assessment, a 360-degree feedback round, or both. It doesn't need to be exhaustive. Even a focused assessment on three or four key behaviours gives you something to measure against later.

The baseline serves two purposes. First, it gives the coach and coachee a shared starting point. "Here's where you are now. Here's where you want to be." Second, it creates a reference for measuring progress down the line. Without it, you're relying on anecdotes and gut feel.

## Repeated assessments over time

A single assessment tells you where someone is. Two or more assessments, spaced across the coaching engagement, tell you whether they're moving.

This is where most measurement approaches fall short. They assess once at the start and maybe once at the end. That gives you a before-and-after snapshot, but it misses the trajectory. Did change happen gradually? Was there a breakthrough moment? Did some behaviours shift while others stayed stuck?

Running the same assessment at regular intervals, say every six to eight weeks, gives you a much richer picture. You can see which areas are responding to coaching and which might need a different approach. The coach can use this data in real time to adjust their strategy, not just to write a final report.

## Self-reported vs observed change

Here's a tension that's worth being honest about: people aren't always great at assessing their own behaviour.

Self-reported assessments are valuable because they capture the coachee's own perception. If someone feels more confident in difficult conversations, that matters, even if external observers haven't noticed yet. Internal shifts often precede visible ones.

But self-reports have blind spots. Someone might believe they've become a better listener while their team would disagree. That's not dishonesty. It's just the nature of self-perception.

This is why 360-degree feedback is so powerful when done repeatedly. Comparing how the coachee rates themselves against how their colleagues, direct reports, and manager rate them reveals gaps. And tracking how those gaps change over time is one of the strongest signals of real behavioural change.

When self-perception and external perception start to converge, something genuine is happening.

## What to measure

Not everything needs to be measured. Trying to quantify every aspect of a coaching engagement creates overhead that nobody wants. Focus on a few things that matter.

**Specific behaviours tied to goals.** If the coaching engagement is focused on delegation, measure delegation-related behaviours. Not general leadership competency, but the specific things the coachee is working on. Keep it tight.

**Frequency and consistency.** Change isn't just about whether someone can do something new. It's about whether they do it regularly. A coachee who delegates well in one meeting but reverts to micromanagement the rest of the week hasn't really changed yet.

**Stakeholder perception.** How do the people around the coachee experience them? This is where repeated 360s earn their keep. A shift in how a team perceives their manager's communication style is concrete, observable evidence of change.

**Coachee confidence and self-awareness.** Self-reported, yes, but still meaningful. When a coachee moves from "I don't know how to handle this" to "I have a plan and I've tried it twice this week," that's progress worth tracking.

## Making it practical

None of this needs to be burdensome. The biggest barrier to measuring behavioural change isn't methodology. It's friction. If running a repeat assessment takes an hour of admin work, it won't happen.

Build measurement into the coaching process from the start. Set the expectation with coachees that they'll complete brief assessments at regular intervals. Use a platform that makes it easy to assign, complete, and compare assessments over time. Keep the assessments focused and short.

The goal isn't to produce a research paper. It's to give coaches better information, give coachees visibility into their own growth, and give organisations genuine evidence that their investment is producing results.

Satisfaction scores will always have a place. But if you want to know whether coaching is actually working, measure the behaviour. Measure it at the start, measure it along the way, and measure it at the end. The story those numbers tell is worth far more than a smiley-face rating on a post-session survey.
