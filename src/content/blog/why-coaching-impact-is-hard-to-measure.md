Ask any coach whether their work makes a difference and they'll tell you yes. They've seen it. The leader who stopped micromanaging. The new manager who found her voice. The executive who finally learned to listen.

The problem isn't whether coaching works. The problem is proving it to the people writing the cheques.

## The anecdote trap

Most coaching programmes rely on testimonials and self-reported satisfaction scores. "I found the sessions really valuable." "My coach was excellent." These are nice. They are not evidence.

Anecdotes tell you someone had a good experience. They don't tell you whether behaviour changed. They don't tell you whether that change stuck. And they definitely don't tell you whether the organisation got a return on its investment.

HR directors and L&D leaders are under increasing pressure to justify spend. "People liked it" is not a business case. Yet that's what most coaching providers hand over at the end of an engagement.

## Why measurement is genuinely difficult

Coaching isn't like training, where you can test knowledge before and after. The outcomes are often behavioural, relational, contextual. How do you put a number on "better self-awareness"?

There are real reasons this is hard:

**Attribution is murky.** A leader improves after six months of coaching. Was it the coaching? The new team structure? The market turning around? You can rarely isolate coaching as the single variable.

**Timescales are long.** Some coaching impact shows up immediately. Some takes months or years. A six-week post-programme survey captures a fraction of the picture.

**Coaches resist it.** Many coaches feel that measurement undermines the trust and psychological safety of the relationship. They worry it turns a developmental conversation into a performance review. This concern is legitimate, but it's also solvable.

**There's no standard.** Unlike therapy, which has validated outcome measures, coaching has no universally accepted measurement framework. Everyone rolls their own, or more commonly, doesn't bother.

## What "good" looks like

Structured measurement doesn't mean turning every session into a data collection exercise. It means being intentional about a few things from the start.

**Set a baseline.** Before coaching begins, capture where the coachee is. Self-assessments, 360 feedback, competency ratings. It doesn't have to be elaborate, but you need a starting point. Without one, you're measuring change against nothing.

**Define outcomes up front.** What does success look like for this engagement? Not vague goals like "develop leadership skills" but specific, observable outcomes. "Delegates effectively to direct reports." "Runs team meetings that finish with clear actions." If you can't describe what success looks like, you can't measure it.

**Measure at multiple points.** A single end-of-programme survey is almost worthless. Capture data at the start, at regular intervals during the engagement, and after it ends. Trends matter more than snapshots.

**Use multiple perspectives.** Self-assessment alone is unreliable. People are bad at evaluating their own behaviour. Combine self-ratings with manager feedback, peer input, or 360 assessments to get a fuller picture.

**Track goal progress, not just completion.** Did the coachee set goals? Did they make progress? Did the goals evolve as the engagement deepened? Goal tracking over time is one of the most practical and least intrusive ways to demonstrate movement.

## The technology gap

Here's what typically happens. A coaching programme runs for six months. Notes live in the coach's notebook or a Word document. Goals are discussed but not tracked anywhere persistent. Assessments happen on paper or in a standalone survey tool. At the end, someone in L&D asks "so, how did it go?" and the coach sends a summary email.

This is not a measurement system. It's a memory exercise.

The missing piece for most coaching practices is a single place where session themes, goals, assessment scores, and feedback data all live together. Not so you can surveil the coaching relationship, but so you can see patterns over time. So you can show a coachee their own progress. So you can give an organisation honest evidence of what changed.

## Start small, start now

You don't need a perfect measurement framework to begin. Pick one thing. Run a baseline self-assessment before the first session. Track three goals per coachee. Repeat the assessment at the midpoint and the end.

Even that basic structure puts you ahead of 90% of coaching programmes. And once you have data, you can refine the approach. Add 360 feedback. Introduce competency frameworks. Segment results by programme or cohort.

The coaches who take measurement seriously aren't the ones who lose trust with their clients. They're the ones who can demonstrate, clearly and confidently, that what they do matters. That's not a threat to the coaching relationship. It's the thing that protects it.
