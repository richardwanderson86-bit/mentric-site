Spend enough time around coaches and you'll hear the framework debate. GROW loyalists. OSCAR evangelists. CLEAR devotees. People who swear by solution-focused approaches. People who think anything structured is antithetical to real coaching.

It's a surprisingly heated conversation for a profession built on asking open questions and withholding judgement.

Here's our take: the framework you use matters far less than whether you use one consistently.

## Frameworks are scaffolding, not the building

GROW (Goal, Reality, Options, Will) has been around since the 1980s. It works. OSCAR (Outcome, Situation, Choices, Actions, Review) adds a review loop. Also works. CLEAR (Contracting, Listening, Exploring, Action, Review) emphasises the relationship upfront. Works too.

They all do roughly the same thing. They give a conversation shape. They stop sessions from meandering. They create a rhythm that helps the coachee move from reflection to action.

No serious research suggests one mainstream framework produces better outcomes than another. What research does suggest is that structured coaching outperforms unstructured coaching. The structure is the point, not the label on it.

## The real differentiator: consistency

Where frameworks earn their keep is in repetition. When a coach uses the same structure across sessions, patterns emerge. You can see whether a coachee consistently struggles at the "options" stage. You can notice when someone always sets ambitious goals but never follows through on actions.

Without consistency, every session is a standalone event. You lose the thread.

This matters even more when coaching at scale. If an organisation is running a programme with ten coaches and fifty coachees, some degree of structural consistency is essential. Not because every session should be identical, but because you need a shared language for talking about what's happening across the programme. "Where are coachees getting stuck?" is an answerable question when there's a common framework. Without one, it's just vibes.

## What actually matters more than the framework

If the framework itself is a secondary concern, what should coaches focus on?

**Capturing what happens.** A framework is only useful if you record what comes out of it. Goals set, actions agreed, themes explored. If it all lives in the coach's head, the framework is just a mental crutch. Write it down. Track it somewhere accessible to both coach and coachee.

**Connecting sessions to each other.** The magic of coaching happens over time, not in isolated conversations. Did the coachee follow through on last session's actions? Has the goal shifted? Are new themes emerging? A good framework supports this continuity. But only if you actually use it to link sessions together.

**Making the data visible.** Coachees benefit from seeing their own patterns. "You've set an action around delegation in three of the last four sessions. What do you think that tells us?" That kind of observation is powerful. It requires records.

**Adapting to the coachee.** Rigid framework adherence is as bad as no framework at all. The best coaches hold a structure lightly. They follow the coachee's energy while maintaining enough shape to keep the conversation productive. If your framework feels like a straitjacket, you're using it wrong.

## The methodology wars are a distraction

There's a version of coach development that focuses heavily on framework mastery. Getting certified in a specific model. Attending advanced workshops on a particular approach. This has value, especially early in a coaching career when you're building confidence and competence.

But past a certain point, the framework becomes invisible. Experienced coaches move through structure fluidly. They don't think "now I'm in the Reality phase." They just coach, and the structure is embedded in how they work.

The coaches who get stuck on framework debates are often avoiding the harder question: am I capturing enough data to actually improve my practice and demonstrate my impact?

## A practical suggestion

Pick a framework. Any reputable one. Use it consistently for six months. Record what happens in each session, specifically the goals, actions, and key themes. Review your notes at the end of six months and look for patterns.

You'll learn more about your coaching from that exercise than from any framework certification. And your coachees will benefit from the consistency whether you call it GROW, OSCAR, or something you made up yourself.

The framework is a tool. Consistency is the practice. Data is the proof. Focus on the last two, and the first one will take care of itself.
