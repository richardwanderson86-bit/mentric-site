There's a pattern in the coaching software market that frustrates us. A talent management platform adds a "coaching" module, slaps some new branding on it, and suddenly it's a coaching platform. It's not. It's an HR tool with a new tab.

This matters more than you think.

## The HR mindset vs the coaching mindset

HR tools are built for oversight. They're designed so that someone in People Ops can track compliance, flag risks, and generate reports for leadership. That's a legitimate need. But it shapes every design decision the product team makes.

When you bolt coaching onto that foundation, the coaching experience inherits all of those assumptions. Sessions become auditable events. Notes become records that feed into performance reviews. The coachee's private reflections sit in the same system that their manager uses to decide promotions.

That's not a coaching relationship. That's surveillance with a softer font.

Purpose-built coaching platforms start from a different question entirely. Not "how do we track what's happening?" but "how do we create a space where real development can happen?"

## What breaks when coaching lives inside HR software

The problems show up fast once you start using these tools with actual clients.

**Confidentiality gets murky.** Coachees don't know who can see what. Even if access controls exist, the fact that their coaching data lives in the same system as their performance data creates doubt. And doubt kills openness. A coachee who's worried about what their manager might read is a coachee who won't be honest about what they're struggling with.

**The workflow is backwards.** HR tools are built around employee records. You find a person, then you find their coaching engagement. A coach thinks differently. You think about sessions, about themes across clients, about which assessments to run next. The information architecture of an HR tool fights against how coaches actually work.

**Assessments feel like performance reviews.** When a 360 or self-assessment comes from the same system that handles annual reviews, people respond differently. They're more guarded. They give the answers they think the organisation wants rather than the honest ones. The data you collect is less useful, even if the questions are identical.

**Reporting serves the wrong audience.** HR dashboards answer questions like "how many coaching hours were delivered this quarter?" That's fine for procurement. But it tells you nothing about whether anyone actually changed their behaviour. Coaches need different metrics: are scores shifting over time, are coachees engaging between sessions, where are the patterns across a cohort?

## What purpose-built actually looks like

A coaching platform built from scratch for coaching has a few things that set it apart.

First, it treats the coaching relationship as the core unit, not the employee record. Everything flows from the engagement between coach and coachee: session notes, goals, assessments, reflections.

Second, it draws hard lines around confidentiality. Coachees can see their own data. Coaches can see their clients' data. Admins and sponsors see aggregate outcomes, not session content. These aren't just permission settings buried in a config panel. They're fundamental to the product's design.

Third, it gives coaches tools that match how they think. Quick access to session history. The ability to spot trends across clients. Assessment workflows that feel like coaching, not like annual reviews. Practice templates you can pull into a session on the fly.

And fourth, it measures what matters. Not just activity metrics, but real indicators of progress. Repeated assessments that show change over time. Self-reported and observed behavioural shifts. The kind of data that helps a coach do better work and helps an organisation understand what it's getting from its coaching investment.

## Why this distinction matters now

Coaching is growing. More organisations are investing in it, and the expectations are higher. People want to see outcomes, not just hours logged. Coaches want tools that make their work easier, not tools that add administrative overhead.

If the platform you're using was designed to solve an HR problem first and a coaching problem second, you'll feel the friction. Your coachees will feel it too.

We're not saying HR tools are bad. They solve real problems. But coaching is its own discipline with its own requirements, and it deserves software that was built with those requirements in mind from day one.

Not as an afterthought. Not as a module. As the whole point.
